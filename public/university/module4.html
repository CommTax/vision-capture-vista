Module 4

Impact — Making It Stick

Clarity gets you understood in the moment. Impact is what survives after the meeting ends.

5 chapters ~25 minutes
Start Chapter 4.1 →

Chapter 4.1

Clear Isn't the Same as Memorable

You can say something perfectly structured, precise, no hedge words, exactly the right order — and still have it disappear from the room within the hour. That's the part nobody warns you about: clarity gets you understood in the moment. It doesn't get you remembered afterward. Those are two different jobs, and most advice about communication stops at the first one.

Think about the last meeting you sat in. You could probably summarize the decisions that got made. Could you say, specifically, who said the thing that changed the direction of the conversation? Most people can't — not because it wasn't said clearly, but because clear things and memorable things aren't built the same way.

Impact is what survives after the meeting ends. It's the sentence someone repeats to their manager afterward, the line that gets quoted back to you weeks later, the thing that makes someone think of you specifically the next time a similar problem comes up.

None of that happens by accident, and none of it requires you to be the loudest person in the room.

Chapter 4.2

Why Nothing Sticks

Most answers fail to stick for one of two reasons, and they're opposites.

The first is sameness — you said something true and reasonable, but so did everyone else, in roughly the same words. Nothing about it distinguished itself from the five other reasonable things said in the same meeting. The brain doesn't file away "reasonable." It files away "different."

The second is flatness — everything you said carried equal weight, so nothing stood out as the important part. If your voice, pacing, and phrasing don't change at all between the throwaway detail and the actual point, the listener has no signal for which one to hold onto. You did the work of having a point. You didn't do the work of marking it as one.

Fixing sameness means saying the thing only you would say — your specific number, your specific example, your actual opinion instead of the safe consensus one. Fixing flatness means literally changing something — a pause before the point, a shorter sentence right at the moment that matters, saying it once, cleanly, instead of three times, softly.

Chapter 4.3

The Specific Beats the General

"This could save us a lot of time" is instantly forgettable, because everyone's already agreed time-saving is good — you haven't added anything a listener can hold onto.

"This cuts our turnaround from nine days to two" is the same claim, and it's the one that gets repeated in the hallway afterward, because it's specific enough to be quoted.

This is the same instinct from Precision, aimed at a different goal. Precision is about not wasting the listener's attention. Impact is about giving them something worth keeping once you have it. A specific number, a real example, an actual name — these aren't decoration. They're the handle the listener grabs onto after you've stopped talking.

Chapter 4.4

Say Less, Weight More

One of the fastest ways to make a point disappear is to give it the same amount of airtime as everything around it. If your opening sentence, your key point, and your closing thought are all delivered at the same pace, in the same tone, with the same sentence length — nothing tells the listener which one mattered.

Try this instead: say the surrounding context at your normal pace. Then, right at the actual point, slow down slightly, shorten the sentence, and let a beat of silence sit right before it.

You're not performing. You're doing the same thing a good headline does for an article — signaling, structurally, this is the part to remember, before the listener has to guess.

Chapter 4.5

The Line People Repeat

Every memorable answer usually has one sentence in it doing more work than the rest — the line someone could repeat word for word if you asked them to, an hour later. Most people never build this on purpose. It just happens to land, or it doesn't.

You can build it on purpose. Before you speak, ask yourself: if they only remembered one sentence from this, which one do I want it to be — and have I actually said that sentence, plainly, out loud, somewhere in there?

Often the answer is no — the sentence exists in your head as an intention, but it never made it into words as a single, quotable line.

Say it. Not buried in a paragraph. On its own.

Notice This

In your next conversation, before you speak, pick the one sentence you'd want repeated afterward — and say it slower, shorter, and more plainly than everything around it.

See if anyone repeats it back to you.
